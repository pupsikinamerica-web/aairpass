THE GOLDEN TICKET / INVESTIGATIONREADING I · ~850 WORDS

How a $400,000 promise became a legal battle

Imagine being able to leave for Paris on a whim, travel to New York for a single meeting and return home before bedtime. No anxious search for discounts, no agonising over ticket prices, no hesitation at the checkout. For Steven Rothstein, an American investment professional, this was not a fantasy. In 1987, he paid American Airlines $250,000 for a remarkable product: a lifetime AAirpass promising unlimited first-class travel. Two years later, he spent another $150,000 on a companion feature. The world had become, in a sense, his personal itinerary.

For the airline, the product had originally seemed like a clever way to raise cash up front. Customers would pay a considerable sum immediately, while the company would provide flights over the following years. In theory, the business could use that money to expand and earn a return. In practice, some customers proved far more committed to flying than the airline had anticipated. The deal turned into a lesson in the dangers of underestimating usage.

Rothstein made extraordinary use of his pass. In July 2004 alone, according to a contemporary investigation by the Los Angeles Times, he took 18 flights, including journeys to London, Miami and other destinations. He acquired tens of millions of frequent-flyer miles over the years. A flight was no longer a rare event that required a special reason; it was an ordinary means of getting around. For some people, that sounds like the ultimate freedom. For others, it raises a question: what happens when travel itself becomes the destination?

His companion feature made the story still more complicated. Rothstein could bring another person on qualifying flights, subject to the terms of his agreement. Over time, airline investigators noticed reservations for fictitious names, such as “Bag Rothstein”, as well as bookings that ultimately went unused. According to the 2011 court ruling, he admitted making reservations using those names. American argued that such practices blocked seats it could otherwise sell and constituted fraudulent use.

Rothstein strongly disputed the airline's interpretation of the arrangement. He argued that staff had long known about — and sometimes tolerated — the booking methods. From his perspective, suddenly applying a stricter interpretation after years of apparent acceptance felt like moving the goalposts. The airline, by contrast, could point to a contract clause allowing it to revoke the pass if it determined the pass had been used fraudulently. Its position was that the companion product was not a licence to reserve an empty seat whenever he wished.

There was also a generous side to his extraordinary mobility. The Los Angeles Times reported that Rothstein sometimes offered surprise first-class upgrades to strangers, including a woman he had met in New Delhi whom he later flew to Chicago. For someone else, his expensive privilege could become a spontaneous act of kindness. It is important, however, not to confuse him with another AAirpass holder, Jacques Vroom, who was reported to have donated frequent-flyer miles to people living with AIDS so they could visit relatives. Their stories overlapped, but they were not identical.

The personal circumstances were more painful than most headlines suggested. In 2002, Rothstein's teenage son, Josh, died following a road accident. In a later first-person article, his daughter Caroline described how airline employees had become a familiar community for her father during an extraordinarily difficult time. She also recalled sometimes resenting his frequent absences when she was younger, while other family members stressed his devotion to returning home. It would be simplistic to declare that planes mattered more to him than his family: travel was linked to joy, belonging, grief and coping, as well as convenience.

On 13 December 2008, the conflict came to a head. As Rothstein and a companion checked in at Chicago O'Hare airport for a flight to London, he was handed a letter telling him the pass had been terminated. According to his daughter's account, he had been helping his companion reach Europe and was left deeply shaken by the abrupt announcement. He later sued the airline for breach of contract. In June 2011, a federal judge ruled in American's favour on that claim. Subsequent proceedings followed, and the dispute eventually ended without his pass being restored.

Yet the facts are more interesting than a simple good-versus-evil story. Was Rothstein exploiting an unclear contract, or was the company seeking a way out of a financially painful promise? Was its conduct within the agreement? And even if a decision is legally permissible, does that automatically make it fair? The answer depends on what evidence we give the greatest weight to.

    06 · Source literacy · 20 minutes

    Evidence is not a verdict.

    A journalist, an airline and a family member can describe the same event very differently without necessarily inventing the underlying facts.

    Version A · The customer

    “An agreement is an agreement.”

    Rothstein believed that the airline's employees had repeatedly allowed the practices now criticised and that the company was trying to back out of an expensive promise. A passenger who has paid in advance, he might argue, deserves predictable and consistent rules.

    Claim / perspective · Not a court finding
    Version B · The airline

    “Unlimited does not mean unrestricted.”

    The airline argued that companion privileges were for genuine companions, not invented names or speculative reservations. Its contract granted a right of revocation for fraudulent usage. According to the company's reasoning, being a heavy user was not itself the contractual violation.

    Claim / position · Tested in court

    07 · A legal grey area · 20 minutes

    Inside the courtroom.

    The legal dispute was not simply about how many flights he took. It was about what the written agreement allowed and how specific booking practices should be interpreted.

    CASE FILE

    Clause 12: Fraudulent Usage

    Educational paraphrase of the contract: If American Airlines decides the AAirpass has been used fraudulently, it reserves the right to revoke the pass and related privileges without refund.

    Question before the court: Did the airline violate the contract by cancelling the pass after reservations were made using fictional companion names?

    The source is the 30 June 2011 decision in Rothstein v. American Airlines, Inc. The wording above is a paraphrase, not a legally authoritative reproduction.

    Four crucial distinctions

    • Validity: Was the agreement legally binding?
    • Scope: Did “companion” include an empty seat?
    • Past practice: Did years of tolerance change the rights involved?
    • Remedy: What was the airline entitled to do if it identified fraudulent use?

    Court outcome: In 2011 the US District Court granted summary judgment for American Airlines on Rothstein's breach-of-contract claim. Do not confuse a civil contract decision with a criminal fraud conviction.

    08 · C1 language mechanics · 20 minutes

    Make your argument harder to dismiss.

    Practise linguistic tools that help you acknowledge uncertainty, concede a point and make an analytical claim without sounding simplistic.

    Concession & qualification

    Admit something without abandoning your position.

    ADMITTEDLY / NEVERTHELESSAdmittedly, the airline misjudged demand. Nevertheless, the contract included restrictions.
    WHILE + CLAUSEWhile the passenger may have felt misled, the agreement did not guarantee unused companion seats.
    EVEN ASSUMING (THAT)…Even assuming the practice was tolerated, its legal status remains debatable.

    Inversion & counterfactuals

    Use advanced structures only when they improve clarity.

    HAD + SUBJECT + PAST PARTICIPLEHad American priced the pass differently, the business risk might have been lower.
    RARELY / NOT ONLY + AUXILIARYRarely had a single ticket created such a long-running dispute.
    IT COULD BE ARGUED THAT...It could be argued that inconsistent enforcement undermined customer trust.

    15

    Hedge, don't exaggerate

    Rewrite the statements in qualified academic English. Try three of the six.

    Absolute → nuanced

    The airline was clearly motivated by greed.

    Emotion → evidence

    He never cared about his family.

    Causation → possibility

    The pass made him addicted to flying.

    Legal overclaim → accuracy

    The judge convicted him of fraud.

    Either/or → complexity

    Either the airline lied or he cheated.

    Overgeneralisation

    Companies always break promises when they lose money.

    1. Financial pressures may have influenced the airline's willingness to reassess the scheme.
    2. His daughter recalled painful absences, although other relatives emphasised his involvement.
    3. The pass may have reinforced an unusually intense attachment to travelling.
    4. A federal judge ruled for the airline on a civil breach-of-contract claim.
    5. The evidence suggests a more complex conflict between customer expectations and the scope of the contract.
    6. Companies may face incentives to reinterpret costly agreements, particularly when terms are open to dispute.
